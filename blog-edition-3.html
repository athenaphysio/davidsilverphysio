Your Brain Is Lying to You — And It's Trying to Help

The most misunderstood thing in healthcare. Why pain is not a damage signal — and why understanding that changes everything.

Dr David Silver PhD
June 20269 min read

The moment

A client came to see me recently. She had been dealing with a persistent back condition for years. Good days and bad, no clear pattern. She asked me why some days felt so different when nothing obvious had changed.

I said:

"You have a day where it is bad. We scan it. Looks the same as yesterday, which was a great day. What has changed? Your brain has gone — I do not know whether we are safe today, we will produce an output. That message is not a pain signal. It is indiscriminate. Your brain makes a judgment call and says: what we are going to do to slow you up a bit, have some pain. That is what pain is. It is a protective mechanism. The sensation is not running parallel to the tissue recovery. When you get to a day that is shockingly bad and cannot work out why — there are thousands of variables. Sleep, stress, thoughts and feelings, hydration."

From the clinic

She sat quietly for a moment and then said: 'the machine is asking the machine?'

I have been thinking about that line ever since.

The insight

Pain is the most universally experienced and most fundamentally misunderstood thing in healthcare.

We have inherited a model of pain that is essentially mechanical. Damage equals pain. It is intuitive and basically logical. However, in a significant proportion of cases, it is wrong.

Pain is not a signal transmitted from an injured body part to a passive brain. It is an output. A decision made by the brain based on a complex calculation about threat and safety.

Two people with identical MRI findings can have completely different pain experiences. One is debilitated. The other has no symptoms at all. The hardware is the same. The software is reading it differently.

In my experience, a client who understands their pain is a protective output rather than a damage signal, relates to their body differently. They become less afraid. And fear is one of the most powerful amplifiers of pain there is.

The bigger picture

Explaining pain neuroscience takes time, skill and judgement. It requires the clinician to be genuinely present, to read whether the patient is onboard, to adjust in real time. Sometimes it can be presented and unpacked in minutes, other times it takes several months.

None of that can be automated. It happens in a relationship, built on trust.

Study after study shows that clients who understand pain neuroscience have better outcomes. Less fear. More movement. Faster recovery.

We have the knowledge — that's for sure. What we often lack is the time to build the relationships, and perhaps the guts to give it.

My client left that session moving differently. Not because anything had changed in her back. Because something had changed in how she understood what her back was telling her.

That is what rehabilitation can be, at its best. Not the management of a structure. The re-education of a system.
