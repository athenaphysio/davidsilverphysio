The Weighing Scales

A simple framework for understanding why some days are agony and others are fine, and what you can actually do about it.

Dr David Silver PhD
August 20269 min read

The moment

A client came to see me recently. Persistent pain, good days and bad days, no clear logic to either.

She had started to believe the bad days meant something was getting worse. That she was, at some fundamental level, broken.

"Why does everything look the same on a scan when some days I can barely move and other days I feel almost normal?"

This is what I said:

"I want to give you a framework. Imagine your pain is like a set of weighing scales. On one side you have dangers. On the other side you have safeties. What gets loaded into the danger basket? Work stress. Immobility. Worry. The fact your mother had the same thing. Everything negative. Safeties are the safety signals — someone trusted explaining it is a sensitivity not damage, keep moving, keep progressing, I trust what I am being told. If the weighing scales are tipped in the danger direction — pain. Your body goes: we are not safe here."

From the clinic

"So it is not about what is physically happening in my back on a given day?"

Not primarily. Not on the days that confuse you.

Why the scales tip

Pain responds to everything. How well you slept. The argument you had before work. The email you are dreading. The memory of the last bad flare. The friend who said, 'oh, I had that, it never really went away.'

"The biggest driver is your system being more sensitive when you are at work because of the stress associated with it. Same body in the same position. Different context. Very different feelings."

From the clinic

The Dubai chair

I want to tell you about a client who went to Dubai. She had persistent symptoms. She went to Dubai for a month. When she came back, she said: I think it was the different chair and desk.

"I said to her: it is not the chair and it is not the desk. It is not even Dubai. It is the context you are in. Your brain went: less threat. You could sit on a beach for twenty hours and feel fine — not because the beach is ergonomically superior but because the danger basket is nearly empty."

From the clinic

I see this constantly. The client who can barely sit through a work meeting but manages a long-haul flight. The client who comes back from holiday moving like a different person.

The trap

"The trap is — I am uncomfortable, I am sore, I am achy. So I stop moving because it hurts more when I do that. And stopping moving loads the danger basket. Which tips the scales further. Which produces more pain. Keep moving, baby. Movement is the only answer because it lets your brain and body know: we are safe."

From the clinic

Immobility is one of the most reliable danger signals the nervous system knows. We are evolutionarily nomadic animals. Prolonged, enforced stillness registers as threat. I think it's odd when it doesn't.

As described brilliantly in the best-selling book, a zebra experiences an acute threat and then the nervous system resets. The cortisol clears. It does not lie awake replaying the chase or catastrophizing about the next one.

We don't work like that. We ruminate. We consume a steady diet of threat signals — news, financial anxiety, the low-grade hum of a stressful job — and our nervous systems treat all of it as real and present danger. It does not disappear when we close the browser. It seeps in.

Every one of those signals goes straight into the danger basket.

Loading the safety side

The practical question is always: what can I load onto the safety side?

Understanding that your pain is a sensitivity, not a structural problem, goes onto the safety side. Trusting the process goes onto the safety side. A change of scene, a class with a good instructor, a conversation that reframes what is happening — all of it goes onto the safety side.

Telling a client they need a new chair loads the danger basket. Explaining that the nervous system is protecting them too enthusiastically, and that the job is to gradually reassure it, loads the safety basket.

Words are clinical tools. Context is a clinical tool. The relationship itself IS the clinical tool!

That is what the weighing scales are really for. Not just to explain pain. To give people something they can actively work with. A question they can ask on a bad day: what has loaded my danger basket today? And what can I do, right now, to add something to the other side?

Keep moving, baby.
