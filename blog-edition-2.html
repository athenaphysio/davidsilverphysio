In the Room with Dr David Silver PhD · Edition Two

The Dyslexic Advantage

The boy on the minibus, the man on the floor in lockdown, and the superpowers nobody thinks to mention.

Dr David Silver PhD
May 20269 min read

The boy on the minibus

I struggled at school with dyslexia very much. And not because I was not a particularly bright kid — I was told I was. But the academic aspects of maths and English, reading and writing, were hugely challenging.

I could not really write my own name until I was eight or nine.

I went to a special educational school at lunchtime, shipped off in a minibus with some other kids. It created a sense of inferiority and challenge that I carried for a very long time. I have only just started to unpack it.

It was not that I didn't know the answers to questions. I didn't even understand the question in itself! That leads to such a great degree of anxiety because you are constantly trying to cover, and justify, and just keep up with something. The emotional energy of that, I now recognise, was such a draining thing. I see it in my daughter now who is going through the same.

It also created a sense of inferiority that I constantly felt I had to justify my capacity.

"That is almost certainly the thing that has driven me to get two undergraduate degrees, a master's degree, and a PhD. And it still sits with me. I prepare religiously for things because I fear being exposed. Quite an irony, as I am now considered a field leader."

Dr David Silver PhD

I have thought about this a lot. What drives someone to grind through four degrees is not ambition in the conventional sense. It is not confidence. It is its opposite. A constant, quiet need to prove that the boy on the minibus was not what the system said he was.

What drove my academic career is a constant need for validation that I am not stupid and I have worth.

I grew up in the very physical world of rugby which suited my energy and bravery. I was accepted for who I am, not for what I was capable of in an exam. On a pitch, the way my brain worked was an asset. Nobody ever looked down at me and made me feel insufficient.

That physical world gave me something the classroom never did: unconditional belonging. And I think that is partly why I ended up spending my career helping people find their way back to physical movement after injury. Because I know fully what it means to have a place where you are simply accepted for what your body can do.

None of this really became apparent to me until the COVID window. I had an opportunity to stop, and take a deep dive, and actually reflect.

The floor in lockdown

I was doing a guided mindfulness exercise (which I am pretty shit at). The voice said: picture yourself at your most vulnerable.

Nothing came to me at first.

Weirdly, as I focused on my breathing, I began to see a young boy sitting at a desk. Dark room, head down. I felt this visceral anxiety grow in me as I lay there. I recognised it was me.

And then the voice said: 'Could you be the person to comfort that individual?'

I burst into uncontrollable tears.

It was almost an out of body experience. And it started to make me, for the first time ever, really unpack the emotions I had as a child around this. Without appearing melodramatic, how traumatic it was. How much of it shaped who I have become, and am still becoming.

Only now, in my forties, am I comfortable sitting in that space.

The superpowers nobody mentions

As I have got older, I have started to realise the strengths that dyslexia has provided me.

The superpowers of communication and visualisation. Of lateral thinking and of deep empathy.

It has given me so much. But because I, like everyone, live in my own skin, I don't have a point of reference. You don't see those things naturally.

What I have come to understand is that the very thing the education system treated as a flaw was quietly building the most important skills I would ever have.

The emotional attunement. The ability to hold complexity without needing to reduce it. The comfort with uncertainty, because uncertainty was where I had always lived.

In twenty years of rehabilitation, these have not been soft secondary skills. They have been my sharpest clinical tools.

What AI gave back

Now I do not type in sessions. I listen. AI has alleviated the administrative struggles that were so time-consuming — the thing limiting my capacity to engage with people in a really powerfully connected way on an emotional level, which I think is my greatest asset.

But the deeper gift is this: because AI now captures what I say in the moment, I can finally see my own thinking. The moving parts that were rattling around in my head for twenty years — I can now understand them. Name them and share them.

Watching it happen again

I am now watching my daughter through similar challenges. I know she will be fine. But it is still painful to see her experiencing the same struggles. The same anxiety. The same exhausting energy of trying to keep up with a system that was not built for the way our minds work.

"I know the boy on the minibus becomes someone who can sit in a room with a person in pain and genuinely understand what they need. I just wish someone had told him that at the time."

Dr David Silver PhD

If you are reading this and you recognise that boy, or you are raising one, I want you to know something;

The thing being treated as the problem, is very often the greatest asset.

The world is changing in your favour. The tasks that disadvantaged you are being absorbed by technology. The tasks that remain — the ones that matter most and that no machine will ever do — are the ones you have been practising your entire life.

You were learning to read rooms. To hold complexity. To stay present with uncertainty. To make people feel genuinely seen.

They are not consolation prizes. They are the real thing.
