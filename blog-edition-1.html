In the Room with Dr David Silver PhD · Edition One

These Words Were Never Meant to Be Written Down

On dyslexia, twenty years of unrecorded insight, and what happens when AI finally lets you see yourself.

Dr David Silver PhD
May 20268 min read

Opening

Let me tell you how this newsletter came to exist.

I am dyslexic. For twenty years, the insights I developed in the clinic, the frameworks, observations, the moments where something clicked for a patient, lived and stayed entirely in the room. I couldn't write them down fast enough. My notes were a mere fraction of any interaction. The insights were given to one person in one moment, and then they were gone.

Then AI note-taking arrived and for the first time, I could see what I actually say.

"Not what I planned to say. Not what I remembered saying. What I actually said. In the moment and unrehearsed, built from years of interactions with people trying to get back to themselves."

From the clinic

This newsletter is what happened next. These are not ideas I sat down and wrote. They are ideas I discovered I already had that have finally been made visible. They are now yours as much as mine.

Where this started

I struggled at school. Profoundly, at times. Dyslexia in the 80s was not well understood to say the least — and I was one of the 'lucky' ones! The classroom was a place where I consistently felt underprepared and exposed, and never quite enough. In hindsight, I was exhausted by the struggle. Not just from trying to keep up, but from covering for my inadequacy.

What the system did have was sport.

On the pitch, none of that mattered. What mattered was how much effort I put in and how I linked with my mates. For the first time in my educational life, I had a space where my brain and body worked as an asset, not a liability.

Sport and exercise became my sanctuary. It still is. It is where I learned what competence felt like. Where I built the confidence the classroom was quietly dismantling. Even now, the thought of running onto a pitch fills me with calm. The release was, and still is, so powerful.

The morning I stopped planning my sessions

Early in my career, I had a ritual. Every morning I would read through my patient notes and plan out each session in detail. I still get in early to ensure I have time. I was prepared. I was organised.

What I did not realise or say out loud — not for a long time — was why.

The anxiety that I had in school had simply followed me into professional life. Same coping mechanism. Different classroom.

Nine times out of ten, the person who walked through my door bore almost no resemblance to the patient I had planned for. The carefully prepared session plan would last about four minutes before I needed to let it go and actually meet the person in front of me.

The Royal Marines have a motto I have carried with me ever since: comfortable with uncertainty. For someone who spent their early life terrified of being caught unprepared, those three words landed like a revelation. It showed how I had to back myself and my skills, not a playbook.

The thing nobody tells you about rehabilitation

Anyone with a smartphone can now access a library of rehabilitation exercises in seconds. The exercises exist. The progressions exist. The rationale is documented and searchable.

The exercises were never really the hard part.

What cannot be Googled is the judgment to know which of those exercises is right for this person, on this particular day, and delivered in this way. Having a library of exercises is like having a kitchen full of ingredients. It does not make a decent meal.

A note on AI — from someone it has transformed

I am not anti-technology. Not even close.

I do not type in sessions. I listen. AI handles the documentation. What that has given back is full presence. Complete and undivided, emotionally available presence. It has been utterly transformative.

But here is the deeper gift. Because AI captures what I say in the moment, I can finally see my own thinking. For the first time in twenty years, the insights that used to live and die in a single session are visible and then, transferable.

This newsletter is the direct result of that.
